Chapter 10 — Glossary

Plain-language definitions of the terms this manual uses. No priors required.

A

Accessibility — Design so materials work for learners across abilities, devices, and contexts: screen readers, grayscale printing, low eye energy, different processing speeds. See Chapter 9.

Active learning — Learning by doing something (answering, teaching back, role-playing) rather than only listening. The part of a lesson plan AI suggests; you pick.

Alt text — Short text describing an image, read aloud by screen readers. Written for function: what the reader would miss without the image.

Analytic grid — A rubric shape with criteria as rows and performance levels as columns. Best for written work with multiple dimensions.

Audience — In prompting: the specific group the output is for, including what they already know. The most-omitted, highest-value instruction in a prompt.

B

Bloom's levels (verbs) — Levels for writing objectives: remember, understand, apply, analyze, evaluate, create. Observable verbs like list, compare, prioritize, justify map to different levels.

C

Citation check — Confirming that a reference an AI produced (a) exists, (b) is described correctly (right content, year, organization), and (c) is current enough to teach from. See Chapter 7.

Closed cast — A prompting technique: give the AI an explicit, fixed list of fictional characters and settings, and forbid inventing anything outside it. Strong protection against real-detail drift in cases and sims.

Constructive alignment — Objectives, activities, and assessment all pointing at the same skill. The teaching principle behind "every segment serves an objective."

D

Debrief — The conversation after a simulation where learning actually sticks. Escalates from what happened → why → transfer to real work.

De-identification — Removing anything that could connect teaching material to a real person: names, dates, places, identifying combinations of details. In this manual's scope, includes near-misses like "the Tuesday patient" and linked "room number + admission date" pairs.

F

Fictional composite — A teaching character built from patterns, not from any real encounter. Must be labeled ("Fictional teaching case") every time.

Facilitator notes — The private half of a scenario: watch-fors, triggers for pausing, and coaching hints. You write more of these yourself than AI does.

H

Hallucination — AI output that is fluent and confidently wrong: invented citations, untraceable statistics, misremembered guideline names. Predictable, routine, and catchable with Chapter 7's five checks.

Handoff — Structured transfer of information between people. In this manual, mostly discussed as an educational skill (clear, complete, confirmed) rather than as clinical protocol.

L

Learning objective — One sentence describing observable learner behavior: condition, verb, standard. The spine of every lesson plan, case, sim, and rubric.

Local tool — AI software that runs on your own machine, with nothing sent to a vendor. Useful when the concern is data leaving your control — though Rule 2 (no real data in the tool at all) is the stronger protection either way.

M

Medical record number (MRN) — A patient identifier. Never appropriate in teaching material or AI prompts. Treated here as a red-flag pattern the provided checker looks for.

Mini-lecture — A short, focused talk (usually 5–15 minutes) inside an active lesson. AI drafts these well when you specify the three talking points yourself.

P

PASS-QC — The review workflow from Chapter 8: Purpose, Accuracy, Safety, Suitability, Quality of language, Care (fairness/accessibility). Designed to run in about ten minutes.

Plain language — Short sentences, common words, jargon defined inline, front-loaded meaning. A teaching skill that AI drafts well with the right constraint prompt.

Prompt — The instruction you give an AI tool. In this manual, structured as: role, task, audience, format, constraints.

Prompt pattern — A reusable prompt skeleton with blanks, like the case or scenario templates in templates/. Patterns transfer between tools.

R

Role card — In simulation: a short card telling one role only what they know, need, and may say. Under 120 words, scannable in 15 seconds.

Rubric — A criteria-by-levels scoring/feedback framework that describes properties of the work, not judgments of the person. See Chapter 6.

S

Single-point rubric — Rubric shape with just one level per criterion — the "target" description — with space for feedback above and below it. Feedback-first, anxiety-friendly.

Simulation (sim) — A structured role-play of a situation for learning, with objectives, roles, facilitation, and debrief. In this manual, kept non-clinical by design: communication, handoff habits, safety culture.

Suitability (S in PASS-QC) — Right level, right room, right prep budget, realistic timing for your actual learners and space.

T

Teach-back — Having a learner re-state learning in their own words to confirm understanding. Test-friendly, low-prep, and hard for AI to fake.

Template — A fill-in form the AI completes inside your structure rather than choosing its own format. The most reliable prompting technique in the manual (see templates/).

[VERIFY] tag — The convention used throughout this manual: any claim an AI marked uncertain must be cleared by a human (checked against a current reputable source), or rewritten as a principle, or deleted.
