Chapter 4 — Case Writing: Fictional, De-Identified, Usable

Teaching cases are the highest-value documents you can draft with AI — because the pattern of a good case is formulaic, and formulaic is where AI shines. The danger is equally real: cases written carelessly can smuggle in real patient details or clinical-decision content. This chapter shows the pipeline that keeps cases fictional, useful, and safe.

The three hard rules for AI-generated cases

  1. Fictional composites only. Built from textbook patterns and plausible human factors, never from a real encounter. If it feels like "that Tuesday patient," stop — that's Rule 2 of Chapter 1.
  2. Education only. The case's learning point is a concept (communication, ethics, teamwork, documentation habits, safety culture), not clinical decision-making, not dosing, not diagnosis.
  3. Label it. Every case opens with: "Fictional teaching case — characters and settings are invented composites with no connection to any real person."

Choose the learning point before the plot

Beginners write "a case about a person"; educators write "a case about a concept wearing a person costume." Decide the concept first:

  • Communication breakdown (handoff, escalation, clarification)
  • Documentation habits (timeliness, precision, correction)
  • Ethics of privacy and disclosure (great for education-only settings)
  • Team dynamics across roles
  • Safety culture: reporting, follow-up, learning-from-near-miss
  • Fatigue, workload, and cognitive load in shift work

Then ask the AI for a situation that exercises exactly that concept.

The fictional detail bank

To help the AI generate nothing identifiable, give it a closed cast list. Prompt pattern:

We will use ONLY these fictional composite characters and settings. Do not
invent names, locations, dates of birth, medical record numbers, or any
identifying details beyond this list.

CAST (all fictional):
- R. Okafor, an experienced evening-shift coordinator at a fictional
  community education center (never a named facility)
- J. Lindgren, a new volunteer coordinator, first week on the job
- Setting: an after-school program that runs medication-adjacent paperwork
  only in the abstract — no real student records
- The concept under study: a rushed verbal handoff that loses one document
  and leads to duplicated work, discovered the next morning

A closed cast lets AI write richly charactered cases (which is the value) while bounding it away from anything real. Swap the cast to your discipline: for nurse-educator sessions, "fictional training sim center staff member R.O." brings clinical texture without clinical stakes.

Structure that keeps cases teachable

Aim for 250–400 words in four layers:

  1. Setting (one sentence, fictional): where, when (weekday/shift), roles.
  2. Friction (two or three sentences): what went wrong or got tangled — build from human and process failure, not diagnosis.
  3. Human response (the size of an iceberg): two to four plausible actions someone took, some right, some questionable. Don't fix it; leave the case unresolved.
  4. Discussion questions (three to five): use verbs from Bloom's levels — identify, contrast, prioritize, justify, propose.

Example skeleton (fictional, non-clinical)

Fictional teaching case — invented composite, not a real person.

Friday evening shift at City Library Annex (fictional). R. Okafor, the
evening coordinator, hands the closing duties to trainee J. Lindgren with
three lines in a text message instead of the standard checklist. Two of the
three are misread because of the abbreviation "ctd" — J. assumes "carts to
desk"; R. means "count the returned-date stamp." The next morning, surveillance
footage review discovers a cart left in the overflow area and one returned-date
stamp unaccounted for until noon.

Discussion questions:
1. Identify every point where the message could have been clarified.
2. Contrast verbal-only handoff with checklist-based handoff in this situation.
3. Propose a two-line checklist text that would have survived abbreviation.
4. Justify why "mark the abbreviation" is or is not enough on its own.

Note deliberately non-nursing props (date stamp, cart) — enough texture to feel real, nothing that walks into clinical-decision territory. For a purely educator-skills session, this is the sweet spot.

Case-writing prompt pattern (the workhorse)

Role: Chair of a fictional committee drafting teaching cases for professional
volunteer coordinators.

Task: Write a 350-word fictional teaching case about [a concept], using only
the cast and setting listed below. Leave the situation unresolved and write 4
discussion questions using Bloom's verbs (two at lower levels, two at higher).

Audience: [students level].

Format:
- First line: "Fictional teaching case — invented composite."
- Paragraph 1: setting (one sentence).
- Paragraph 2: the friction.
- Paragraph 3: human actions taken (2–4).
- Then: "Discussion questions:" list.

Constraints: No names beyond the cast, no real institutions, no dates with
more than day-of-week precision, absolutely no real patient or student data of
any kind, no medication decisions or doses of any kind. Education-focused; the
teach point is [concept]. Mark any factual claim [VERIFY].

Generate three versions, pick the best, merge what's good from the other two. Generation is cheap; judgment is not — so spend judgment at exactly the step where it buys the most.

De-identification pass with human eyes

Even after AI, run the eyeball test:

  • Specifics creep. "a 36-year-old with a Tuesday med-surg admission" — that's a fingerprint a colleague could recognize; cut or genericize it. Weekday and role textures ("Friday evening shift") are fine — they're atmosphere, not identity.
  • Tool-test creep. If any line of your case implies a specific real facility, system, form, or company, genericize it ("the center's intake form").
  • Reality test. Ask one colleague to read it cold: "Could this be an actual event at us?" If yes, fictionalize something visible.

What to do when the AI drifts clinical

Written mostly as-is above, cases tend to stay put. But if a correction or a line slides into dosing, diagnostics, or clinical guidance — the manual stops being educational and starts being something it must never be — take the low road: cut the line, not a rewrite. Restate the same educational mechanism (clarification, escalation, documentation, teamwork) in non-clinical terms and have the AI re-draft just that line. This keeps you commercially and ethically clean: education-only, always.

The case revision loop that actually saves time

  1. Draft with the workhorse prompt (3 versions).
  2. Fact-pass: everything tagged [VERIFY] checked against current, reputable sources; anything fragile gets rewritten as a principle, not a stat.
  3. De-identification pass by eyeball + the provided checker.
  4. Read-aloud pass: any sentence you stumble on, rewrite shorter.
  5. Usability pass: can your least-experienced student finish it in 6 minutes? If not, cut one character or one sentence of friction.

Ten minutes total, and your case is classroom-ready.
