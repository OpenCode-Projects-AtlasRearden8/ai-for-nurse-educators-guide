Chapter 7 — Citation and Hallucination Checks

AI invents citations the way a confident stranger gives directions: smoothly, plausibly, and wrong. This chapter gives you a repeatable process for catching fabricated references, invented numbers, and "realistic" guideline names before they end up in front of students.

Why AI hallucinates (the 30-second explanation)

Language models generate plausible text, not verified text. When asked for a citation, it assembles a pattern that looks like a citation — real-sounding author, real-sounding journal, real-looking year — without looking anything up. The output can be 90% pattern and 10% reality. Citations, statistics, and guideline names are exactly the content types where pattern beats reality.

The three hallucination types to hunt

  1. Fabricated citation. Author + journal + year all plausible; article doesn't exist. Sometimes it's a mash-up of two real papers.
  2. Real source, wrong content. A real guideline exists — cited for a claim it doesn't make, or from the wrong year/edition.
  3. Plausible number. A specific percentage or incidence figure with no verifiable origin. Numbers fabricated by AI almost never trace to a real source, and "40% of students report…" is the classic case.

The prevention prompts (use before generation)

Add to any prompt that could touch facts:

Verify before stating: if you are not certain a fact, guideline name, or
citation is real and current, either omit it or mark it [VERIFY]. Do NOT
generate citations from memory. If I need references, list search terms I
can use to find them myself instead of inventing sources.

That last line is the trick: ask for search terms, not sources. AI is honest (and useful) at generating search strategy; it is unreliable at generating bibliography.

The verification pass (use after generation)

Five checks, in order, 30–60 seconds each:

  1. Existence check. Paste the title into a real search engine, a library database, or a citation database. If you can't find the exact article in 2 minutes, treat it as fabricated. (Tip: search the title in quotes plus the first author's surname.)
  2. Content check. Open the real source. Does it actually support the sentence in your draft? Wrong-content hallucination is sneakier than fabrication.
  3. Freshness check. For any guideline, standard, or statistic, check the publication year against today. Guidelines change; a 2012 edition handed to students is a teaching error.
  4. Attribution check. Does the AI attribute the claim to the source's actual author or organization? A real article cited to a made-up organization is still a hallucination.
  5. Number check. Trace every statistic to a named, current, reputable source — or delete the number. A principle stated in plain words is more teachable than a suspect percentage.

Worked example: catching a fabricated citation

Suppose an AI draft includes this line:

"Studies show that 68% of learners prefer visual case formats (Morales & Chen, 2019, Journal of Applied Health Pedagogy)."

Verification walkthrough:

  • Existence: search the exact title/author pair → not found; the journal name sounds plausible but doesn't appear in a library catalog. Fabricated.
  • Even if "Journal of Applied Health Pedagogy" existed, the number 68% is untraceable. Delete.
  • Rescue the sentence by restating it as a principle not a stat:

"Many learners report preferring visual case formats; consider offering a visual version alongside the text version."

Same pedagogical utility, zero fabrication risk.

Rendering uncertainty honestly in your teaching materials

When you can't verify something but it's pedagogically useful:

  • Generalize: "commonly reported," "widely taught," "varies by setting."
  • Attribute to yourself as the educator: "In my experience in the simulation lab…"
  • Name the uncertainty for learners: "Numbers vary by source and year; your clinical agency's policy is the source of truth for practice."

Students respect uncertainty stated plainly. They lose respect fast for authority that gets the details wrong.

Citing AI use itself (transparency default)

Whenever an AI tool contributed to material students will see — even just as a first-drafter — a short disclosure line protects everyone:

"Drafted with the assistance of an AI writing tool; reviewed and edited by the instructor. Fictional teaching material — not clinical guidance."

For student-facing AI assignments, mirror the standard you set: require students to disclose AI use the same way. One sentence of transparency prevents a semester of ambiguity.

A note about this manual's own citations

You'll notice this manual cites almost nothing numerically. That's deliberate. Where a claim depends on a specific number, guideline name, or study, we've _stated it as a principle_ instead — because a manual that round-trips through AI generation itself must model the restraint it teaches.
