Chapter 3 — Lesson Plans with AI

A lesson plan is just a promise you make to yourself: by the end of this session, learners will be able to do X, and here's how we'll get there. AI is excellent at the connective tissue — timing, transitions, activity shapes — and bad at deciding what matters in your course. You decide the destination; the AI paves the road.

Anatomy of a lesson plan (the parts AI can help draft)

PartWhat it isAI draft quality
Learning objective(s)Observable, measurable, learner-levelGood per draft; needs verb-leveling
Hook / anticipatory setOpens the session, grabs attentionGood — expects editing
Content outlineTalking points, key conceptsGood — needs fact check
Active learning activityPractice, discussion, simulationMostly human-critical; AI can frame
Materials & prep listWhat you need in the roomVery good
Assessment / exit ticketHow you'll know objective was hitGood, one exit ticket per objective
Timing mapMinutes per segmentVery good
Access & inclusion notesHow the plan works for all learnersGood starting notes

Step 1: Fix the objective first

Garbage objectives in, garbage plans out. A usable objective:

  • Names an observable learner behavior (list, demonstrate, compare, prioritize, teach-back) — not "understand" or "appreciate"
  • Has a condition ("given a fictional case," "in the skills lab") and often a standard ("in fewer than 5 steps," "per the rubric in this packet")

Then have the AI pressure-test it:

Here is my objective: "Students will be able to prioritize the first three
actions for a workstation interruption during a skills check-off." Is this
observable? Which Bloom's level is it? Suggest a rewording if it is not
measurable, and list three activities that would fit it. Fictional teaching
material only; mark facts [VERIFY].

Level your objectives across a unit too: a good block mixes lower-level (Objective 1: list the steps) and higher-level (Objective 2: prioritize among competing tasks) verbs. Ask the AI to tag each of your objectives with its Bloom's level; most do this reliably.

Step 2: Generate the plan against your template

Open templates/lesson-plan-template.md, copy it, and prompt:

Role: nurse educator drafting teaching materials.
Task: Fill in the attached lesson-plan template for a 50-minute class session
on professional communication in a simulated handoff.
Audience: second-year associate-degree nursing students; they have seen one
simulation day.
Format: keep my template's exact section order; fill every [bracket].
Constraints: Education only. No drug names, doses, or clinical-decision
guidance. All details fictional and generic; no patient data of any kind.
Mark every factual statement [VERIFY]. Bias toward low-prep, low-cost
activities using only paper, whiteboard, and chairs.

You'll get a draft in under a minute that's maybe 70% right. Your job: fix the segments that don't fit your room, replace generic activities with your favorites, and clear every [VERIFY].

Step 3: Fix the four classic AI lesson-plan defects

  1. Time collapse. AI happily assigns 4 minutes to an activity needing 15. Redo the timing map yourself against the real clock.
  2. Activity inflation. Proposes "think-pair-share plus gallery walk plus Kahoot" for a 50-minute room. Keep one active-learning segment and make it good.
  3. Objective drift. Activities that don't map to any objective. For each segment, ask: which objective does this serve? If the answer is silence, cut it.
  4. Comma-spice inspiration. Overwritten hooks ("Imagine a world where…"). Strip to one clean question or scenario line.

Step 4: Build the assessment last

One exit-brief-check per objective beats one big quiz. Prompt pattern:

For each objective below, write ONE exit-ticket item: either a 2-sentence
constructed response or a 3-option multiple choice with plausible distractors.
Audience: [yours]. Fictional only, no clinical decision guidance, tag factual
claims [VERIFY].
Objectives:
1. …
2. …

Check the distractors — AI distractors are either absurd or barely different. Good distractors are plausible misconceptions your students actually hold. Edit until you recognize your students in them.

Step 5: Accessibility pass (early, not at the end)

Now — not the night before — run the Chapter 9 checklist:

  • Objective written in plain language? Read it aloud; if you stumble, rewrite.
  • Any image-only instructions? Add text equivalents.
  • Timed activity with no flexibility? Add a no-timing alternative.
  • Print copy legible in grayscale? Re-run tools/check_no_patient_data.py and your own eyeballs.

Worked micro-example (fictional)

Objective: Given a fictional scenario card, the learner will teach back three priority handoff elements in under two minutes.

  • Hook (5 min): "What gets lost when a verbal handoff is rushed?" Chart on board, no answers corrected yet.
  • Mini-lecture (10 min): Three talking points — what a handoff carries [VERIFY], what breaks first under time pressure (human factors, fictional composite) [VERIFY], and the teach-back move.
  • Activity (25 min): Pair role-play with two fictional scenario cards (Scenario A: new head of a school soccer club needing paperwork handoff; Scenario B: fictional clinic reception desk shift change — deliberately everyday transfer practices, no clinical decisions).
  • Debrief (5 min): Two prompts: what did your partner do that helped? What felt rushed?
  • Exit ticket (5 min): Teach-back of the three priority elements.

Deliberately non-clinical props keep the discussion on communication skill, not on clinical urgency — safer, and it generalizes.

Checklist before you teach it

  • ☐ Objectives observable and leveled
  • ☐ Every segment serves an objective
  • ☐ Timing map survives a real clock
  • ☐ Facts marked [VERIFY] are actually verified
  • ☐ No patient data anywhere (run the checker)
  • ☐ Accessibility pass done
  • ☐ You would be happy if a colleague observed this plan exactly as written
