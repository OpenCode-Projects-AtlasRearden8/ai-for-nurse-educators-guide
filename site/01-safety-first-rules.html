Chapter 1 — Safety First: The Non-Negotiable Rules

Read this chapter once, slowly. Everything else in the manual builds on it.

Rule 1: Education only, never clinical advice

This manual — and everything you create with it — is about teaching materials. It is never:

  • Guidance for treating an actual patient
  • A substitute for institutional policy, protocols, or order sets
  • A source for drug dosing, calculations, or clinical decision-making
  • A replacement for your board of nursing, accreditation, or curriculum committee

If a learner's clinical question could affect real patient care, the answer comes from your policy manual, your preceptor, and your faculty — not from an AI chat, and not from this book.

Rule 2: No real patient data, ever

This is the biggest one. Never paste real patient information into any AI tool. Not "just this once," not "the AI is private," not "I removed the name." Real patient details belong in the medical record, not in a chat box.

Banned from any prompt, ever, even partially:

  • Names, initials, nicknames
  • Dates of birth, ages paired with admission dates, admission/discharge dates
  • Medical record numbers, account numbers, room and bed numbers
  • Employers, addresses, phone numbers, email addresses
  • Any story specific enough that a colleague could recognize the person ("the 36-year-old post-op from Tuesday night shift" — that's identifying)
  • Photographs, screenshots, wound images, scan images

Say it in categories instead: a middle-aged adult with new shortness of breath is fine. A 54-year-old named patient from Tuesday is not.

Everything in this manual's examples uses clearly fictional composites built from textbook patterns, and every template in templates/ includes a "Fictional — not a real person" line. The included tool tools/check_no_patient_data.py scans your files for common real-data patterns (MRN-like numbers, DOB-style dates, URL-safe identifiers) before you share or commit anything.

If you have already pasted patient data into a chat tool: follow your organization's privacy-incident procedure immediately. Tell your privacy officer. Do that first; recover later.

Rule 3: The AI is a draft generator, not an authority

Language models predict plausible text. That means they can produce content that is:

  • Fluent and completely wrong (a made-up normal value)
  • Half right (a real guideline cited from the wrong year)
  • Confidently invented (a journal article that does not exist)

Treat every AI draft like a nursing student's strong first attempt: promising, never signed off without review.

Rule 4: Verify before a learner sees it

Before anything leaves your desk:

  1. Facts and numbers → check against a current, reputable source.
  2. Guidelines → confirm the year and organization.
  3. Citations → confirm the article actually exists (Chapter 7).
  4. Tone → read it once out loud; AI writing has a tells-and-hedges rhythm.
  5. Clinical accuracy → your professional judgment; you are the safeguard.

Rule 5: Keep grading and progress decisions human

AI can draft rubric wording and suggest feedback phrasing. The decision about a real learner's progress — grades, remediation, readiness — stays with qualified humans, following your institution's process. Never feed a real learner's identifiable work to an AI tool without checking your institution's policy on student data, intellectual property, and accessibility.

Rule 6: Check your tool's terms — once, carefully

Before using any AI service with course material, skim:

  • Does the vendor train on your inputs? (Many can be toggled off.)
  • Is there an education or privacy tier?
  • If learners will use it, is there a minimum age and what data is collected?

When in doubt, prefer local tools (running on your own machine) for drafting sensitive-adjacent content — see Chapter 2 — or simply keep identities and records out of the tool entirely, which is what this manual teaches.

Quick audit: the 30-second safety checklist

Before you hit Enter on any prompt, ask:

  • ☐ Am I typing only fictional/category-level details?
  • ☐ Would this still work if I replaced every specific with "a sample"?
  • ☐ Is my goal here teaching material, not clinical guidance?
  • ☐ Am I prepared to verify whatever comes back?

Yes × 4 → proceed. This habit is the whole chapter in one breath.
